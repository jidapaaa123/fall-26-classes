- Question/Problem: "How far do you go down with abstractions?" 
- When Identified: 8/28/26 11:45am
- Importance (1-5): 4. Immediately applicable to anyone who produces code.  
- How to Learn: Participating in the "How far down do you go with abstractions? - Jonathan Lopez" session at the Unconference  
- Insight/Answer: First the discussion began about code abstractions as expected. Someone mentioned that some abstractions may be leaky, sometimes by design. It's leaky in the sense that it does not encapsulate all the detail and requires you to know something. An example of this leaky abstraction was a car: it encapsulates a lot, but you still need to know how to get in the car and use it. In terms of level of abstraction, a key question to consider is "is the effort required to abstract this function worth it, given how much the use case will change or will be used at all?" The conversation sidetracked to the usage of AI and "abstraction" in form of technical jargon.

- Question/Problem: Where has AI 'been' and where is it going? 
- When Identified: 8/28/26 12:45pm
- Importance (1-5): 3. Hate it or love it or something in between, I can't escape AI. It's good to know its context and projected trajectory  
- How to Learn: Participating in the "AI Retro - Ellis Lempriere" session at the Unconference  
- Insight/Answer:  
  - Reflect on inventions of calculator, GPS, and other technology. Analogy to forest fires: "forest fires aren't bad to the forest. It's bad for the humans and our property. But a forest fire every now and then is needed to clean up the woods / etc. and still provide compost and nutrients for the land" or something like that. Basically, a way of saying it's a way of purging something old to make way for something new. Every invention we take for granted now, had a group of people offended and fearmongered at its inception. Some might have been valid, but we had learned how to live around it or use it smoothly(?)   
  - Someone brought up a really good point about the business executives making AI out to be magic. They do love to put it in everything and shove it into everyone's faces.   
  - My subjective opinion: I agree with the notion that people had, historically, consistently got mad at new technology. That being said, that doesn't invalidate any criticism about AI. But I also don't think that means every criticism about AI is inherent about AI itself. For example, I personally think LLM's should not have been released to non-engineers so early on. While engineers/tech people commit "AI sins" too, we are aware of it and, most of all, conscious of the fact that AI is not magic nor is it perfect enough to have it be the end-all-be-all mechanism. It's the non-technical group that commit those sins without being aware or informed about how LLM's work. Parasocial behavior towards AI bots formed due to this, and subsequently reliance on AI for everything, including it being used as a proxy for human connection. It allows people, especially youth in education, to get away with "being stupid" because AI can generally pass those classes for them. The classes meant to build their critical thinking at the foundational stage.    
  - Joy and intimacy with the code sacrificed for time and competition constraints. I miss being intimate with the code that I write. But that privilege comes with a lot of time investment. With the popularity of AI, it had been that professors expect me to use them and, thus, push out more code. That works in theory but fails miserably if it results (and often, it did) in just overload of work that took any time away from in-depth, slow learning at all. For people in the industry, I mentioned that this would translate into them needing AI to help with code to keep up with market competition's speed, even if that meant they don't truly feel connected to the code that they write, just understood it enough at best.
